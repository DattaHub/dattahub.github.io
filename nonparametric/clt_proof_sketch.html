The Central Limit Theorem

Theorem (CLT). Let \(X_1, X_2, \ldots\) be IID with mean \(\mu\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2 > 0\). Then

\[Z_n = \frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty\]

Proof strategy. It suffices to show that the moment generating function (MGF) of \(Z_n\) converges to \(e^{t^2/2}\) for all \(t\), since \(e^{t^2/2}\) is the MGF of \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\).

This follows from a theorem in analysis: if \(M_{X_n}(t) \to M_X(t)\) for all \(t\) in a neighbourhood of zero, then \(X_n \xrightarrow{d} X\).

What is a Moment Generating Function?

Definition. The moment generating function (MGF) of a random variable \(X\) is

\[M_X(t) = \mathbb{E}(e^{tX}) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{tx} f(x)\, dx\]

for all \(t\) in some open interval around zero where the expectation is finite.

–

Why “moment generating”? Differentiating \(k\) times and evaluating at \(t = 0\) recovers the \(k\)-th moment of \(X\):

\[M_X^{(k)}(0) = \mathbb{E}(X^k)\]

For example: - \(M_X'(0) = \mathbb{E}(X)\) - \(M_X''(0) = \mathbb{E}(X^2)\), so \(\text{Var}(X) = M_X''(0) - [M_X'(0)]^2\)

–

Why do we use it in the CLT proof? Two reasons:

  1. Uniqueness. If two random variables have the same MGF in a neighbourhood of zero, they have the same distribution.

  2. Independence. If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, \(M_{X+Y}(t) = M_X(t) \cdot M_Y(t)\). This is the key factorisation used in Step 2.

MGF: Two Examples

Example 1. \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\).

\[M_X(t) = \mathbb{E}(e^{tX}) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{tx} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2/2}\, dx = e^{t^2/2}\]

This is why \(e^{t^2/2}\) appears as the target in the CLT proof: it is simply the MGF of the standard normal.

–

Example 2. \(X \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p)\).

\[M_X(t) = \mathbb{E}(e^{tX}) = (1-p)\, e^{t \cdot 0} + p\, e^{t \cdot 1} = 1 - p + p e^t\]

Check: \(M_X'(0) = p e^t \big|_{t=0} = p = \mathbb{E}(X)\). \(\checkmark\)

–

Limitation. The MGF does not always exist. For example, the Cauchy distribution has \(\mathbb{E}(e^{tX}) = \infty\) for all \(t \neq 0\). In such cases we use the characteristic function \(\varphi_X(t) = \mathbb{E}(e^{itX})\), which always exists since \(|e^{itX}| = 1\).

Step 1: Standardise the Summands

Define centred and scaled versions of the \(X_i\):

\[Y_i = \frac{X_i - \mu}{\sigma}\]

Then \(\mathbb{E}(Y_i) = 0\), \(\text{Var}(Y_i) = 1\), and the standardised sum becomes:

\[Z_n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \sum_{i=1}^{n} Y_i\]

Let \(M_Y(t) = \mathbb{E}(e^{tY})\) denote the common MGF of each \(Y_i\).

We assume \(M_Y(t)\) exists in some open interval around \(t = 0\).

Step 2: Factor the MGF Using Independence

\[M_{Z_n}(t) = \mathbb{E}\!\left(\exp\!\left\{t Z_n\right\}\right) = \mathbb{E}\!\left(\exp\!\left\{\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}} \sum_{i=1}^{n} Y_i\right\}\right)\]

Since \(Y_1, \ldots, Y_n\) are independent:

\[= \prod_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb{E}\!\left(\exp\!\left\{\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}} Y_i\right\}\right) = \left[M_Y\!\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right]^n\]

This step uses independence in a critical way. Without it, the MGF does not factor, and the proof breaks down.

Step 3: Taylor Expand the MGF

Expand \(M_Y(s)\) in a Taylor series about \(s = 0\):

\[M_Y(s) = M_Y(0) + s\, M_Y'(0) + \frac{s^2}{2} M_Y''(0) + O(s^3)\]

Recall that \(M_Y(0) = 1\), \(M_Y'(0) = \mathbb{E}(Y) = 0\), and \(M_Y''(0) = \mathbb{E}(Y^2) = \text{Var}(Y) = 1\). Therefore:

\[M_Y(s) = 1 + \frac{s^2}{2} + O(s^3)\]

Now substitute \(s = t/\sqrt{n}\):

\[M_Y\!\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}}\right) = 1 + \frac{t^2}{2n} + O(n^{-3/2})\]

Step 4: Take the Limit

Substituting back:

\[M_{Z_n}(t) = \left[1 + \frac{t^2}{2n} + O(n^{-3/2})\right]^n\]

Using the standard limit: if \(a_n \to a\) then \(\left(1 + \dfrac{a_n}{n}\right)^n \to e^a\),

\[M_{Z_n}(t) \to e^{t^2/2} \quad \text{as } n \to \infty \qquad \square\]

Since \(e^{t^2/2}\) is the MGF of \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\), we conclude \(Z_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1)\).

Conditions

The proof requires three things:

  1. Existence of the MGF near \(t = 0\), so the Taylor expansion in Step 3 is valid.

  2. Independence, used in Step 2 to factor the joint expectation into a product.

  3. Finite variance, used in Step 3 to identify the \(s^2/2\) coefficient.

–

What the proof does not need:

Any specific distributional form for \(X_i\). The shape of the parent distribution completely disappears by Step 4. This is why the result is universal.

–

Limitation. The MGF need not exist for all distributions – for example, heavy-tailed distributions such as the Cauchy have no finite moments at all.
The rigorous general proof uses characteristic functions \(\varphi(t) = \mathbb{E}(e^{itX})\) instead, which always exist, and the argument is identical in structure.

Connection to Nonparametric Statistics

The CLT will reappear throughout this course as the justification for large-sample approximations to p-values in rank-based tests.

For example:

  • The Wilcoxon signed-rank statistic \(T^+\) has a known exact distribution under \(H_0\), but for large \(n\) we use a Normal approximation via the CLT.

  • The Wilcoxon rank-sum statistic \(W\) similarly converges to Normal under \(H_0\).

  • The runs statistic \(R\) converges to Normal under the null of randomness.

In each case, the CLT applies to a sum of (approximately) independent terms, and the proof structure is the same as above.

Nonparametric methods often provide an alternative: exact finite-sample results based on ranks, which do not require the CLT at all. These exact results are one of the main advantages of nonparametric inference for small sample sizes.
