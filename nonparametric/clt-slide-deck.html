The Central Limit Theorem

Jyotishka Datta

Virginia Tech

Today’s question

Suppose the population is skewed, discrete, or otherwise very non-Normal.

Why does the sample mean often have an approximately Normal distribution?

By the end, you should be able to:

  • state the Central Limit Theorem;
  • distinguish a population distribution from a sampling distribution;
  • compute probabilities for sample means, sums, and proportions;
  • judge when a Normal approximation is reasonable.

Three distributions—not one

  1. Population distribution: the distribution of one observation, \(X\).
  2. Sample distribution: the values \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) in one observed sample.
  3. Sampling distribution: the distribution of a statistic, such as \(\bar X_n\), over repeated samples.

The CLT is a statement about the sampling distribution of a standardized sum or mean.

A thought experiment

Start with a strongly right-skewed population:

If we repeatedly average \(n\) independent observations, what shape will those averages have?

Repeated sampling

The center stays at \(\mu=1\), the spread shrinks, and the shape becomes more Normal.

The Central Limit Theorem

Let \(X_1,X_2,\ldots\) be IID with

\[ E(X_i)=\mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_i)=\sigma^2<\infty. \]

Then

\[ Z_n=\frac{\bar X_n-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n} \xrightarrow{d} N(0,1) \qquad \text{as }n\to\infty. \]

Equivalently, for sufficiently large \(n\),

\[ \bar X_n \mathrel{\dot\sim} N\!\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right). \]

The population itself does not become Normal. The distribution of the standardized mean does.

What changes as \(n\) grows?

For \(\bar X_n\):

\[ E(\bar X_n)=\mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}, \qquad \operatorname{SE}(\bar X_n)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}. \]

  • Center: remains at \(\mu\).
  • Spread: decreases at rate \(1/\sqrt n\).
  • Shape: becomes approximately Normal under the CLT conditions.

Doubling \(n\) does not halve the standard error. To halve it, multiply \(n\) by \(4\).

The standardized picture

A practical CLT workflow

For a probability involving a sample mean:

  1. Identify \(\mu\), \(\sigma\), and \(n\).
  2. Check independence and whether the sample size is plausible.
  3. Use \[\bar X\mathrel{\dot\sim}N\!\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right).\]
  4. Standardize the boundary: \[z=\frac{\text{boundary}-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}.\]
  5. Use \(\Phi\), a Normal table, or pnorm().
  6. Interpret the probability in context.

Worked example: coffee fill volume

A machine fills cups with mean \(\mu=12.0\) oz and standard deviation \(\sigma=0.6\) oz. Individual fills are somewhat skewed. For a random sample of \(n=36\) cups, approximate

\[P(11.9<\bar X<12.2).\]

The standard error is

\[ \frac{0.6}{\sqrt{36}}=0.1. \]

Therefore,

\[ P(11.9<\bar X<12.2) \approx P\left(-1<Z<2\right) =\Phi(2)-\Phi(-1). \]

Sums are covered too

Let \(S_n=X_1+\cdots+X_n\). Since \(S_n=n\bar X_n\),

\[ \frac{S_n-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n} \xrightarrow{d}N(0,1), \]

so

\[ S_n\mathrel{\dot\sim}N(n\mu,n\sigma^2). \]

For a mean, the standard deviation is \(\sigma/\sqrt n\).
For a sum, the standard deviation is \(\sigma\sqrt n\).

Worked example: total demand

Daily demand has mean \(42\) units and standard deviation \(9\) units. Assume demands on different days are IID. Over \(50\) days, approximate

\[P(S_{50}>2200). \]

Under the CLT,

\[ S_{50}\mathrel{\dot\sim}N(50\cdot42,50\cdot9^2) =N(2100,4050). \]

Thus

\[ z=\frac{2200-2100}{9\sqrt{50}}\approx 1.571. \]

Bernoulli variables and sample proportions

If \(X_i\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p)\), then

\[ \bar X=\hat p, \qquad E(\hat p)=p, \qquad \operatorname{SE}(\hat p)=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}. \]

The CLT gives

\[ \frac{\hat p-p}{\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}} \mathrel{\dot\sim}N(0,1). \]

A common rule of thumb is that both \(np\) and \(n(1-p)\) should be at least about \(10\) for a comfortable Normal approximation.

De Moivre–Laplace: Binomial CLT

If \(X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)\), then

\[ \frac{X-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}} \xrightarrow{d}N(0,1). \]

Therefore,

\[ X\mathrel{\dot\sim}N\bigl(np,np(1-p)\bigr). \]

Because \(X\) is discrete and the Normal distribution is continuous, a continuity correction usually improves the approximation.

Continuity correction

Let \(Y\sim N(np,np(1-p))\). Translate integer outcomes into intervals of width \(1\):

Binomial probability Normal approximation
\(P(X\le k)\) \(P(Y\le k+0.5)\)
\(P(X<k)\) \(P(Y<k-0.5)\)
\(P(X\ge k)\) \(P(Y\ge k-0.5)\)
\(P(X>k)\) \(P(Y>k+0.5)\)
\(P(X=k)\) \(P(k-0.5<Y<k+0.5)\)

Memory aid: move the Normal boundary outward so that it covers exactly the integer outcomes you want.

Binomial example

Let \(X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(25,0.4)\). Approximate \(P(X\le8)\).

\[ \mu=np=10, \qquad \sigma=\sqrt{np(1-p)}=\sqrt6. \]

With continuity correction,

\[ P(X\le8) \approx \Phi\!\left(\frac{8.5-10}{\sqrt6}\right). \]

How large must \(n\) be?

There is no universal cutoff.

  • Nearly symmetric, light-tailed populations: modest \(n\) may work well.
  • Strong skewness: larger \(n\) is needed.
  • Rare-event Bernoulli data: check both \(np\) and \(n(1-p)\).
  • Heavy tails: convergence may be very slow.
  • Infinite variance: the classical CLT does not apply.

“\(n\ge30\)” is a rough classroom mnemonic—not a theorem.

Conditions and warning signs

The classical version assumes:

  • observations are independent;
  • observations are identically distributed;
  • the common mean exists;
  • the common variance is finite.

Be cautious with:

  • time series or spatial dependence;
  • a few extreme observations;
  • samples dominated by one cluster;
  • very skewed populations with small \(n\);
  • Cauchy-like heavy tails.

What the CLT does not say

  • It does not say the raw data are Normal.
  • It does not guarantee a good approximation for every \(n\).
  • It does not make dependence disappear.
  • It does not justify ignoring infinite or unstable variance.
  • It does not say \(\bar X\) is exactly Normal—unless the population is Normal.

Quick check

A population has mean \(80\) and standard deviation \(24\). For samples of size \(64\):

  1. What is the approximate distribution of \(\bar X\)?
  2. What is \(P(77<\bar X<85)\)?
  3. If the sample size becomes \(256\), what happens to the standard error?

Answers:

\[ \bar X\mathrel{\dot\sim}N(80,3^2), \]

\[ P(77<\bar X<85)\approx\Phi(5/3)-\Phi(-1), \]

and the standard error falls from \(3\) to \(1.5\).

Takeaways

  1. The CLT concerns the distribution of a standardized sum or mean.
  2. For large \(n\), \[\bar X\mathrel{\dot\sim}N\!\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right).\]
  3. The mean stays centered at \(\mu\) while its standard error shrinks as \(1/\sqrt n\).
  4. Sums, proportions, and Binomial counts are direct applications.
  5. Independence, finite variance, skewness, and tail behavior determine whether the approximation is credible.
